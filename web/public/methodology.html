How this forecast works

A Monte Carlo election model in the tradition of poll-plus-fundamentals forecasters. It models every 2026 Senate race (35), governorship (36), and House district (435), refreshes itself every two hours, and — most importantly — reports how uncertain it is alongside every number.

What we measure

For each race we estimate the Democratic-minus-Republican margin on election day and the probability the Democrat (or Dem-caucusing candidate) wins. Chamber toplines count seats across correlated simulations: 218 for the House; 51 for the Senate, where the 34 Dem-caucus seats not up this cycle (including the independents who caucus with Democrats) are the baseline and the VP tie-breaker is Republican.

The pipeline

every 2 hours (Cloudflare Worker cron)
│
├─ POLLS        VoteHub API (race-level) · Wikipedia race articles (redundancy feed,
│               cross-source deduped) · Silver Bulletin sheet (generic ballot)
├─ CANDIDATES   FEC filings (federal) · Ballotpedia (governors, primary results,
│               automatic nominee calls — incl. RCV and post-primary withdrawals)
├─ MONEY/ECON   FEC receipts · FRED (unemployment, inflation, sentiment)
│
▼
MODEL RUN (every 2 hours, 10,000 simulations)
1. average    weighted poll average per race: recency decay, sample size,
              pollster quality (Silver Bulletin ratings), in-model house-effect
              estimation, partisan-sponsor and RV/LV adjustments; polls testing
              primary losers are excluded once a nominee is called
2. prior      fundamentals: partisan lean (2024 presidential results on the
              2026 maps, reconciled to certified totals) + 0.8 × generic ballot
              + incumbency + relative fundraising
3. blend      precision-weighted: sparse/stale polling → fundamentals dominate;
              heavy fresh polling → polls dominate (each race reports its split)
4. simulate   correlated Monte Carlo: shared national error (Student-t, fat
              tails) + regional errors + per-race noise → win probabilities,
              80% margin intervals, seat distributions

Why correlated simulation matters

Races are not independent: if polls are wrong in Michigan they're probably wrong the same way in Wisconsin. Every simulation draws one shared national polling error (fat-tailed, so 2016- and 2020-sized systematic misses occur at their historical frequency — the cycle-level polling error 1998–2024 has a standard deviation of about 3 points), plus regional and race-level noise. This is why a candidate up 3 points on election eve is a ~73% favorite here, never 99%.

Backtest

The statistical core (poll averaging, house effects, blending, and the error model) is run as an election-eve forecast against every Senate and governor's race from 2006–2022 with at least two late polls — 411 races across 9 cycles — and scored against actual results (data: Silver Bulletin rawpolls archive). This backtest runs in CI on every change to the model.

Brier score 0.069 (0 is perfect; always-guessing-50% scores 0.25) · 80% intervals covered the actual margin 81.0% of the time · mean absolute margin error 4.8 points. The first version of the error model was wider than history justified (89% coverage); the race-level noise term was re-fit so stated intervals mean what they say.

Honest limits: the backtest exercises the polls-driven path (historical partisan leans aren't in the archive), its error constants were calibrated on the same archive's aggregate statistics, and the House model's district-level noise floor is not separately backtested — district polling is too sparse historically. Mid-range underdogs (the ~16–33% buckets) have won slightly less often than priced; the sample there is small (~25 races per bucket) and we haven't corrected for it.

Known limitations

  • Crossover incumbents. Priors are presidential-lean-based, so governors like Vermont's (a Republican who wins a deep-blue state) and House members who outrun their district's lean are systematically mis-priced until polling arrives. This is our largest known gap versus other models.
  • Ranked-choice dynamics. Alaska's top-four/RCV races are modeled as plain D-vs-R; other forecasters treat them as much closer than our lean-based read.
  • Independents. Races are reduced to a Dem-caucus vs Republican margin; a serious third candidacy isn't separately modeled.
  • Coefficients. Incumbency (±2.5), elasticity (0.8), and sponsor adjustments are literature-standard values, not fitted; the backtest validates the polling path, not these.
